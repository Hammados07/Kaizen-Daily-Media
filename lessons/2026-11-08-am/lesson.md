# Why a quiz beats a re-read

**Hook.** Two groups of students studied the same science passage. One group read it again. The other group closed the page and tried to write down what they remembered. A week later, in one experiment, the group that tested itself recalled 61 percent. The group that re-read recalled 40.

### The idea

Re-reading feels good because the words look familiar. Familiar is not the same as known. Your eyes slide over the page and your brain says "yes, I have seen this", and it stops working.

Pulling an answer out of memory is different. It is effortful, and the effort is the point. Each time you fetch a fact, the path to it gets stronger and easier to find next time. Researchers call this the testing effect, or retrieval practice. The odd part is that people who re-read rate their own learning higher, yet remember less.

Spacing adds a second lever. If you test yourself a few days later, when the fact has started to fade, the fetch is harder, and the gain is bigger. That is why today is a review. Five lessons went out since Thursday morning. Let us see what stayed.

### A short history

In the 1880s a German psychologist, Hermann Ebbinghaus, tested only himself. He memorised lists of meaningless syllables, tested himself after different delays, and published in 1885. Forgetting is steepest right after learning, then it slows. So the best moment for a second look is when you have just begun to forget, not right away. A fetch that takes a little struggle tells the brain the fact is worth keeping.

A blank is not failure. It is where the learning happens, as long as you check the answer and try again. A simple rhythm: test the next day, then three days later, then a week later.

### Worked example: this week's five questions

Answer each one out loud before you read the answer.

1. **The 200-millisecond conversation.** About how long is the typical gap between turns in conversation? *Answer: about 200 milliseconds, a fifth of a second.*
2. **One letter changes 125 of 256 bits.** How many bits is a SHA-256 fingerprint, and how many hex characters? *Answer: 256 bits, 64 hex characters.*
3. **The Morse code drawn in sand.** What did Norman Joseph Woodland borrow from Morse code? *Answer: the idea of short and long marks, stretched into thin and thick lines.*
4. **A 99% alarm that is right 17% of the time.** A spam filter catches 95% of spam and wrongly flags 2% of good mail. If 1 mail in 100 is spam, is a flagged mail more likely spam or good? *Answer: in 10,000 mails, 95 spam are caught and 198 good ones are flagged. So a flagged mail is more likely good. About 32% of flagged mail is spam.*
5. **The rice that survives the pot.** What is the bacterium, and what is a spore? *Answer: Bacillus cereus. A spore is a tough, dormant shell that survives cooking and wakes when conditions are warm and wet.*

Score yourself, zero to five. A miss is useful: it marks the exact fact that needed the extra fetch. Count of lessons sent in the past 7 days: 5.

### Try it now (5 minutes, no screen)

On the commute, pick one lesson from this week and teach it to an imaginary friend in three sentences. No notes. Where you stall is the gap.

Check: if you could say the key number or name, it is held. If you stalled, say the answer once, then try again without help.

### The memory trick

Think of memory as a footpath through grass. Re-reading is looking at the path from a bridge. Recalling is walking it. Only walking wears the grass down.

### Use it this week

Pick one thing from your own work, such as a formula, a process step or a client name, and quiz yourself on it at the end of the day, then again two days later. Two fetches, not ten re-reads.

### Three quick questions

1. What is the testing effect in one sentence?
2. Why does re-reading feel more effective than it is?
3. What does spacing add on top of testing?

Answers: (1) Pulling a fact out of memory strengthens it more than looking at it again. (2) The text looks familiar, which feels like knowing. (3) A harder, later fetch, which builds a stronger memory.
