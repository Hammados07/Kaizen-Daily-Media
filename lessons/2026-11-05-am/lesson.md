# The 200-millisecond conversation

2026-11-05, morning. Theme: People skills.

Listen: https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Hammados07/Kaizen-Daily-Media/main/lessons/2026-11-05-am/lesson.mp3

**Hook.** When you answer a question, you start speaking about a fifth of a second after the other person stops. That is faster than you can blink twice. Yet a pause of four seconds can make someone feel left out. Your whole social life runs inside that gap.

### The idea

In 2009 a team of researchers recorded everyday conversations in ten languages, from Danish and Dutch to Japanese, Korean and a Mexican language called Tzeltal. They measured the gap between one person finishing and the next one starting. The gap was short everywhere. The typical answer came about 200 milliseconds after the question ended. Japanese speakers were fastest, with almost no gap at all. Danish speakers were slowest, at about half a second. Even the slowest was quick.

How can anyone answer that fast? Thinking of a reply and getting your mouth ready takes longer than 200 milliseconds. So you start preparing your reply while the other person is still talking. You guess where their sentence will end, and you aim for that moment. In a normal chat, you stop listening early. You are busy with your own line.

In 2011 a Dutch team asked about 160 students to watch a short video of three women talking, and to imagine they were one of them. In one version, after a remark, the group went quiet for four seconds. In the other, they moved on at once. The students who sat through the four-second silence felt more rejected and less sure they belonged. No one said anything unkind.

Conversation has a very tight rhythm. We expect the next turn almost at once. When it does not come, we read it as a message about us.

### A worked example

A colleague says: "I think we should move the review to Friday."

Habit A: you hear "move the review" and begin to build your objection. You jump in at 0.2 seconds. You answer what you expected, not what was said. Maybe she was about to add "so everyone can prepare."

Habit B: you let her finish, count one beat in your head (about one second), then say back the main point: "So Friday gives people time to prepare?" The cost is under a second. The gain is that she feels heard, and you have the whole sentence.

A normal gap is 0.2 seconds. Your beat is 1 second. The awkward limit in the study was 4 seconds. You can pause five times longer than usual and still be far from the danger zone.

### Why it matters

Think about the conversations where you felt most listened to. Chances are the other person did not rush. They let you finish, took a small breath, and showed you they had caught your point.

When you wait for the full sentence, you often hear the real concern at the end. People tend to put the main point last, after a few warm-up words. The one-beat pause is not only polite. It makes your answer better.

And if you are met with silence, remember that most of the time the other person is simply thinking. You do not have to fill every gap, and you do not have to read it as a verdict on you.

### Try it now (on your commute, no screen)

Listen to one real conversation near you, or to a podcast voice. Do not judge it. Just estimate the gaps. Do speakers start before the other has finished? Do they ever leave a whole second?

Check: you will notice overlaps and very short gaps, and a gap of one second feels surprisingly long. That is the 200 millisecond rhythm at work.

### The memory trick

Think of a tennis rally. A good player watches the ball until it leaves the other racket, and only then moves. **Watch the ball until it leaves the racket.** Listen until the sentence has left their mouth. Then count "one" and hit it back.

### Use it this week

In your next conversation at dinner or at work, try the one-beat pause once. Then say back the main point in a few words. Do it once a day and notice what changes in what they say next.

### Three quick questions

1. About how long is the typical gap between turns in conversation?
2. How do we manage to reply that quickly?
3. How long did the silence last in the 2011 study, and how did students feel after it?

Answers: (1) About 200 milliseconds, a fifth of a second. (2) We prepare our reply while the other person is still talking, and aim for the moment we predict they will finish. (3) Four seconds. They felt more rejected and less like they belonged.
