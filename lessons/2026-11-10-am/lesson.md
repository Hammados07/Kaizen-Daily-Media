# Why you cannot tickle yourself

**Hook.** Your fingers are just as good at tickling as anyone's. The skin under your arm is just as sensitive as it was when your cousin made you scream. Yet you cannot tickle yourself. In 1998 a London team built a machine to find out why, and added a delay of a fraction of a second. The delay brought the tickle back.

### The idea

Every time you move, your brain does two jobs. It sends the command to your muscles. It also keeps a quiet copy of that command, called an efference copy. The copy is used to predict what the movement will feel like. If you stroke your own palm, the brain already knows when and where the touch will land, and how strong it will be.

When the real touch matches the prediction, the brain turns the volume down. Most of what you feel while you move is caused by you: your clothes shifting, your feet on the floor, your tongue in your mouth. If you felt all of it at full strength, you could not notice the things that matter, like a stranger's hand or an insect on your skin. A tickle from someone else is a surprise. There is no copy, so no prediction, and the touch arrives at full strength.

In 1998, Sarah-Jayne Blakemore, Daniel Wolpert and Chris Frith tested this. People moved a lever with one hand, and a robot arm with a foam tip stroked their other palm. When the robot copied the move at once, people rated the touch as less ticklish than the same touch with no link to their move. When the experimenters delayed the robot, the ratings rose as the delay grew, and by about 200 milliseconds the touch felt much more ticklish. A brain scan in the same paper agreed: self-made touch produced less activity in a touch area of the cortex, and part of the front of the cerebellum, the brain's timing and prediction centre, went quiet.

The Conversation article on this work also reports that people with schizophrenia who have delusions of control could tickle themselves with no delay. The researchers read this as a failure to compare the command copy with the real touch. That is one study's reading, not a medical statement.

### A worked example

Invented ratings on a 0 to 10 tickle scale. Robot touch with no delay: 3. Delayed by 100 ms: 5. Delayed by 200 ms: 7. A light stroke from another person: 8. The tickle rises 4 points from a 200 ms wait, with the same foam and the same stroke. Only the timing changed. So the tickle lives in the gap between what you expected and what arrived.

### Try it now (no screen)

On the commute, notice one touch you cause yourself: your hand on a rail, your bag strap, your feet on the floor. Then notice one you did not cause: a bump from a passenger, a gust of wind. Compare how loud each one feels.

**Check:** the touch you did not cause will almost always stand out more. Your brain muted the other one before you noticed.

### The memory trick

**"Copy, predict, mute."** The brain keeps a copy of the command, predicts the touch, and mutes it. A surprise means no copy, so no mute. Picture a sound engineer who turns down every sound you made yourself.

### Use it this week

Next time there is a tickle fight with a child or family member, notice that a tickle from an unexpected spot lands harder than one they see coming. You cannot do it to yourself, and that is the lesson.

### Three quick questions

1. What is an efference copy?
2. Why did adding a delay to the robot make the touch more ticklish?
3. What is the memory trick?

**Answers.** 1. A copy of a movement command that the brain uses to predict the result of the movement. 2. The delay broke the match between prediction and touch, so the brain stopped turning the sensation down. 3. Copy, predict, mute.
