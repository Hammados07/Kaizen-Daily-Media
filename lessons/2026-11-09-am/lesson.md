# The shop that grew broke while making a profit

**Hook.** A business can make a profit every month and still close because it ran out of cash. It happens more often than most people think, and the reason is a number almost nobody checks: the days between paying for goods and getting paid for them.

### The idea

Profit and cash are not the same thing. Profit is a score kept on paper. Cash is what you can spend today. A sale counts as profit the day you make it, even if the customer pays next month. A purchase of stock is not a cost until you sell it, even though the money has already left your account.

So there is a gap, and you can measure it in days. It is called the cash conversion cycle. It has three parts.

- Days of stock: how long goods sit on your shelf before they sell. Call it DIO.
- Days to get paid: how long customers take to pay you after a sale. Call it DSO.
- Days to pay: how long your suppliers let you wait before you pay them. Call it DPO.

The formula is **DIO + DSO − DPO**. The first two add up the days your money is stuck. The third is the credit your suppliers give you for free, so it is taken away.

If the answer is positive, you pay out cash first and wait to get it back. Every extra rupee of sales needs extra cash from you, or from a lender. If the answer is negative, customers pay you before you pay your suppliers. Growth then brings cash in. Supermarkets and many online shops work this way: the customer pays at the till, and the supplier is paid weeks later.

This is why fast growth can kill a healthy business. The more it sells, the more cash it needs up front.

### A worked example (invented numbers)

Two shops each buy goods worth 1,000 rupees a day.

Shop A supplies lunches to offices. Its stock lasts 10 days. Offices pay after 30 days. Its suppliers want payment after 15 days. Cycle = 10 + 30 − 15 = **25 days**.

Shop B is a small grocery. Its stock lasts 12 days. Customers pay at the till, so 0 days. Its suppliers allow 30 days. Cycle = 12 + 0 − 30 = **−18 days**.

Now both shops double their buying, adding 1,000 rupees a day. Shop A must find about 25 × 1,000 = **25,000 rupees** of extra cash to carry the growth. Shop B gains about 18 × 1,000 = **18,000 rupees** of cash. Same profit margin, opposite stories. Shop A can go broke while winning. Shop B funds its growth from its suppliers. (Rounded teaching numbers; real accounts use yearly averages.)

**Three levers.** A shop can shorten its cycle by holding less stock (risk: running out), asking customers to pay sooner (risk: they walk away), or asking suppliers for longer terms (risk: a higher price). Every day saved in one place is paid for somewhere else. Before any big order, ask: when does the cash leave, and when does it come back? If the gap is long, plan who pays for those days.

### Try it now (5 minutes, no screen)

On your commute, pick any shop you pass. Guess its three numbers. Does it hold stock for days or for weeks? Do customers pay on the spot or later? Do you think its suppliers wait? Decide if its cycle is likely positive or negative.

**Check:** A tea stall that pays milk suppliers weekly and sells for cash likely has a short or negative cycle. A firm that sells to other businesses on 60-day invoices likely has a long positive one. A cash sale is the strongest sign of a short cycle.

### The memory trick

Picture a **water pipe**. Stock and slow customers are water you pour in at the start. Supplier credit is water someone else pours in for you. The cycle is how many days the pipe stays full before water comes back out. Say: **"Stock plus slow customers, minus slow payments out."** DIO and DSO are what you wait for. DPO is what you borrow.

### Use it this week

Look at your own household. Which bills do you pay before the money arrives, and which arrive before you pay? Paying a card on the last allowed day is your personal DPO. If you front cash for a family trip, the days until you are repaid are your DSO.

### Three quick questions

1. What is the formula for the cash conversion cycle?
2. Which of the three parts is subtracted, and why?
3. Why can a fast-growing shop with a positive cycle run out of cash while profitable?

**Answers.** 1. DIO + DSO − DPO. 2. DPO, because it is credit from suppliers, so it shortens the time your own money is stuck. 3. Each extra sale needs more cash tied up in stock and unpaid bills before any money returns.
